How long does it take to build an app?

The honest answer is that it depends, but not in the evasive way that phrase usually means. The phases are predictable. What varies is how much lives inside each one.

The phases are not the surprising part

Every app build moves through the same four stages: working out what it is, designing it, building it, and getting it through review and onto the store. The proportions shift, but the sequence does not.

What surprises people is where the time actually goes. It is rarely the part that looks hardest.

Scoping is short and decides everything

Working out what version one is takes days, not weeks, and it saves more time than any other part of the process. Most overruns trace back to a decision that was never made, not to code that was slow to write.

If nobody has written down what the app does not do, scoping has not happened yet.

Design takes longer than people expect

Designing an app is not decorating it. It is deciding what happens when the list is empty, when the network drops, when a field is filled in wrong, and when someone opens the app for the first time with no data at all.

Those states are most of an app's real surface area, and skipping them does not save time. It moves the work into the build, where it costs more.

The build is the most predictable part

Given a settled design, development is the phase that estimates well. Working builds should be on your device throughout, not at the end. If you are not seeing progress you can hold in your hand, something is wrong with the process rather than the timeline.

Review is short but not instant

App Store review is usually days rather than weeks, but a rejection resets the clock, and first submissions get rejected more often than experienced teams admit. Budget for at least one round.

Most rejections are avoidable and predictable, which is worth knowing before you submit rather than after.

What reliably adds weeks

Changing the scope mid-build. Waiting on content, logins or access to a third-party system. A stakeholder who has not seen anything until the end. Integrations with a service whose documentation turns out to be wrong.

Notice that only one of those is technical. Most schedule risk is coordination, which is why a build with clear decisions moves faster than a build with a bigger team.

If you want a real timeline rather than a range, that is what scoping produces.
